When the air conditioning quits in Wilson, NC, the house tells you fast, and so does everyone in it. The part that fails most often is the humble run capacitor: a few dollars of electronics that starts the compressor and fan, weakens quietly for years, and finally lets go on a brutal afternoon, leaving the outdoor unit humming but the fan still. Right behind it come pitted contactors that no longer pass power cleanly, condenser fan motors with dry bearings, refrigerant leaks that slowly bleed the system's capacity away, and evaporator coils that ice over solid whenever airflow collapses behind a clogged filter.

At Wilson HVAC, every AC repair starts with measurement. Capacitors are metered against the rating on the label. Amp draws on the compressor and fan motor are compared to the data plate. Refrigerant charge is judged by superheat and subcooling, not by pressure readings alone, and any icing prompts a look at the evaporator coil, blower wheel, and filter. This matters because a single warm-air symptom can hide four different faults at four very different prices, and a refrigerant top-off that ignores the leak is just a short-term rental on cold air.

Honest advice is part of every visit. If an aging system's compressor has failed, we explain the repair-versus-replacement math plainly and let you decide. Most of the time, though, the fix is simpler, and because our trucks stock capacitors, contactors, fan motors, hard-start kits, and condensate pumps, the house is usually cooling again before we leave. Warning Signs for AC Repair

The sooner you notice these red flags, the easier and cheaper the problem is to resolve. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • A burning smell or repeated breaker trips when the AC starts
  • The outdoor unit humming or clicking but the fan not spinning
  • Water dripping from the indoor unit or showing up on the ceiling below
  • Ice on the refrigerant lines or a frozen indoor coil
  • Vents blowing air that is barely cooler than the room
  • Rapid on-off cycling that never brings the temperature down
  • Cooling bills climbing while the house stays sticky and warm
  • A hiss or gurgle from the lines, a common sign of leaking refrigerant

The Benefits of Professional AC Repair

Start with the refrigerant. It lives in a sealed, pressurized loop, venting it is prohibited under federal rules, and correct service requires gauges, a vacuum pump, and a recovery machine rather than a store-bought recharge kit. Add capacitors that hold a dangerous charge with the power off and a condensing unit fed by 240 volts, and the risks add up quickly. Then there are the diagnostic traps: an iced coil often points to poor airflow rather than low charge, and topping off a system with an unrepaired leak lets moisture and acid slowly damage the compressor while your money leaks out with the refrigerant.

A professional repair looks at the whole system, not just the symptom. Our technicians meter electrical parts before replacing them, make charge decisions from measured superheat and subcooling, locate leaks with electronic detectors and dye, and check the filter, blower, and static pressure any time airflow could be the issue. The result is a repair that lasts the season instead of a guess that fails in three weeks. If your system is not worth saving, we will tell you directly, backed by numbers rather than a sales pitch.

AC Repair Prevention Tips

Smart maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • Leave vents open in every room, since closing them strains the blower
  • A new buzz or click at startup often means a weak capacitor, so call before it fails
  • Hose off the condenser coil every spring and trim plants to at least two feet away
  • Have the AC serviced every spring, ahead of the first real heat
  • Swap filters monthly while the AC is running hard to keep the evaporator coil from icing
  • Start each cooling season by flushing the condensate line so algae cannot block it
  • Never enclose the condenser; it needs open space on every side to shed heat
